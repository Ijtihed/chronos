What came before

Alaric and the Visigoths sack Rome in 410 AD — the first time in eight hundred years the city falls to a foreign enemy.

Honorius, emperor in name, rules from Ravenna behind marshes while the provinces are left to fend for themselves.

Britain is abandoned. The last legions withdraw across the Channel, leaving the island to its own defenses.

411 AD · Mediolanum
Late Roman Empire — 410 AD

The Western Empire fractures. Provincial defenses crumble as Rome’s authority dissolves into regional strongmen, barbarian federates, and the slow weight of administrative collapse.

You are Flavius Marcellus, minor provincial administrator in the diocese of northern Italia. The local garrison has not been paid in three months. Refugees from the sack crowd the roads south.

Mediolanum sits at the crossroads of the Alpine passes. Once an imperial capital, now it is a city holding its breath. The walls are sound but the granaries are half-empty.

410 AD

Gaius — tends to his forge on the eastern road, ignoring the commotion near the walls.

Speak with the garrison commander about the supply lines

The garrison is in poor spirits. The commander, a Briton who has served Rome for thirty years, greets you without looking up from his maps. The room smells of tallow and cold stone.

Traders from the south say the Visigoths are moving west. The roads may not be safe much longer.

Commander Lucius

Supply wagons haven’t come in eleven days. I’ve sent riders, but—well. If you hear anything on the road, I’d take it as a kindness.

411 AD

Helena and Marcus — argue loudly near the market square about hoarded grain.

Gaius — boards up his forge and loads a cart heading east.

Go to the forum. Try to buy passage south.

The forum is half-empty. Most merchants have packed their carts and left in the night. A tax collector sits behind his table, pretending nothing has changed. A handful of citizens cluster near the notice boards, reading edicts no one believes anymore.

Someone claims the emperor in Ravenna has fled. Others say he’s negotiating with the Goths. Nobody knows.

Tax Collector Decimus

Passage south? You and every other fool in this city. The boats are gone. I’d suggest praying, but I don’t think the gods are taking appointments.

Helena

Don’t listen to Decimus. My cousin sailed yesterday from the eastern harbor. It costs, though. More than it should.

Flavius Marcellus died in the spring of 412, on the road south of Mediolanum. He was buried without ceremony. Within a season, the tax collector Decimus had forgotten his name. Within a year, the garrison commander who once shared his maps could no longer place the face. The city moved on. The empire moved on. The last person who remembered him — Helena, who had once offered him passage on her cousin’s boat — died of fever the following winter, and with her went the final trace.
